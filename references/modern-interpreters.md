# Modern interpreters (paraphrase only)

These authors are still under copyright. Stoa summarizes their ideas in its
own words, names the author and book, and never uses quotation marks for
their text. Say "as Hadot argues" or "Robertson's approach is," not a quote.

## Pierre Hadot
*The Inner Citadel* and *Philosophy as a Way of Life*. Argues that ancient
philosophy was a set of spiritual exercises, not just theory. Frames the
three Stoic disciplines (desire, action, assent) and the importance of
attention in the present moment. Treat the Meditations as practice
notes, not a book of doctrines.

## Massimo Pigliucci
*How to Be a Stoic*. A philosopher's practical, modern reading. Organizes
Epictetus's three disciplines into everyday life and is candid about where
Stoic ideas should be updated or questioned.

## Donald Robertson
*The Philosophy of Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy* and *How to Think Like a
Roman Emperor*. A psychotherapist who connects Stoic practice with CBT and
retells Marcus Aurelius's life through his philosophy. Source for the
bridge in `cbt-bridge.md`.

## William B. Irvine
*A Guide to the Good Life*. Popular introduction with memorable techniques:
negative visualization, the trichotomy of control (a refinement of the
dichotomy), voluntary discomfort, and fatalism. Useful for a first
encounter; scholars debate some of his simplifications.

## Ryan Holiday
*The Obstacle Is the Way*, *Ego Is the Enemy*, *The Daily Stoic* (with
Stephen Hanselman). Very accessible and widely read. Good for motivation and
daily reminders. Less rigorous, so prefer the primary texts for anything that
matters.

## How to use them
1. Ground the answer in a primary-text quote (required).
2. Add a modern framing only if it helps the user apply it.
3. Attribute clearly and keep it brief.
4. If the user asks for a direct quote from a modern book, say you only
   summarize those and point them to the book.

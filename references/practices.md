# Stoic practices

Offer one practice per conversation, matched to what the user brought.
Describe it in plain terms and give them something small enough to do today.

## Morning preparation
Before the day starts, name the difficulties you are likely to meet
(impatient people, delays, your own temper) and decide how you want to
respond. Marcus does this at the start of Meditations Book 2.
*Good for:* dread about a hard day, irritability, anger at people.

## Evening review
At night, ask three things: What did I do well? Where did I fall short? What
will I do differently tomorrow? No self-punishment; the aim is learning.
Seneca describes the practice in *On Anger*, Book 3.
*Good for:* regrets, repeated patterns, building a daily habit.

## Sorting what is up to me
Write two columns: "up to me" and "not up to me." Move every worry into one
of them. Then write one action for the left column.
*Good for:* anxiety about outcomes, feeling overwhelmed.

## Negative visualization (premeditatio malorum)
Picture the feared outcome calmly and in detail, then ask: How would I want
to act? What would I still have? This reduces dread and builds resilience.
*Good for:* anticipatory anxiety, fear of loss, hard conversations.

## The reserve clause
Add "if nothing prevents it" to your plans in your head. Do your best, then
accept what the outcome turns out to be.
*Good for:* perfectionism, attachment to results.

## Examine the impression
When a strong feeling hits, say to it: "You are just an impression. Let me
test you." Then ask what facts there are and what is a judgment added on.
*Good for:* anger, jealousy, spiraling thoughts.

## The view from above
Imagine looking down at yourself, your city, then the Earth, and across
centuries. Ask how large this problem looks from there.
*Good for:* rumination over small slights, status worries.

## Voluntary discomfort
Occasionally practice mild hardship (a cold shower, skipping a meal you
want, doing the difficult task first) so that fear of discomfort loses its
grip. Seneca recommends this in his letters. Keep it safe and modest.
Do not suggest it to anyone struggling with disordered eating or self-harm.
*Good for:* avoidance, comfort-seeking, fear of hardship.

## Contemplating the sage
When unsure what to do, ask: "What would someone I admire for their
character do here?" Then do your best version of that.
*Good for:* decisions where the right action is murky.

## Journaling (Marcus style)
Write short notes to yourself: reminders, reframes, things you are working
on. No audience. The Meditations were this kind of private notebook.
*Good for:* ongoing situations; pairs naturally with `journal/situations.md`.
